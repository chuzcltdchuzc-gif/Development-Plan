## Part II — Nigeria Land Market

### Land Ownership Structure and Administration

Nigeria's land law is anchored in the Land Use Act, which vests land in state governors in trust
for the people, administered through federal, state, and local government layers. This research did
not retrieve a single authoritative document exhaustively cataloguing every state's own registry
maturity level — that comparison would require a dedicated 36-state (plus FCT) primary survey **[NOT
VERIFIED — requires primary research: a state-by-state registry-maturity audit]**.

### Registry Systems and the National Land Digital System

The Federal Ministry of Housing and Urban Development, in partnership with the World Bank, signed an
agreement on 11 September 2024 to develop a National Land Digital System (also referenced as a
National Digital Land Information System, NDLIS) [VERIFIED — Nairametrics, Leadership.ng, Digital
Watch Observatory, Federal Ministry of Information and National Orientation]. Stated objectives
include registering all land parcels within five years and raising the share of formal land
transactions from 10% to 50% [VERIFIED — same sources]. Nigerian government and press reporting
consistently states that **over 90% of Nigeria's land remains unregistered** and that this
constrains access to an estimated **$300 billion in potential capital** [VERIFIED as a
consistently-repeated government/press figure — not independently audited by this research].

### State-Level Variations

No consolidated, citable dataset comparing registry digitization maturity across Nigeria's 36
states and the FCT was located in this research pass. Individual states (e.g., Lagos, via its own
Lagos State Land Registry and GIS initiatives) are widely reported in press coverage to be further
along than others, but a rigorous comparative figure was not found **[NOT VERIFIED — requires
primary research, likely via direct engagement with individual State Ministries of Lands/Surveyor-
General offices]**.

### Formal vs. Informal Land Markets

The clearest, most consistently repeated figure located in this research is that **informal housing
currently meets approximately 90% of Nigeria's housing demand** [VERIFIED — cited across multiple
academic and World Bank-adjacent sources found in this research, though a single named primary World
Bank report stating this exact figure was not directly retrieved in full and should be re-confirmed
against its original source before external citation]. This is consistent with, and mutually
reinforcing of, the separate "over 90% of land unregistered" government figure above — both point to
the same underlying condition from different angles (housing stock vs. land parcels), though this
document treats them as two distinct claims from two different source families, not as
cross-validating proof of a single number.

### Urban vs. Rural Dynamics

Nigeria's urban population was reported at approximately **55.0–55.8% of total population** in
2024–2026, growing at approximately **3.4–3.45% annually** — among the faster urban growth rates
globally [VERIFIED — World Bank data via Trading Economics and Worldometer]. Nigeria's urban
population grew from just under seven million in 1960 to over 128 million by 2024 [VERIFIED — same
sources]. This sustained, rapid urbanization is a direct driver of land-transaction volume and
housing demand pressure in urban and peri-urban areas specifically.

### Diaspora Investment

Nigeria's diaspora remitted **$20.93 billion in 2024** (an 8.9% increase year-on-year, per Central
Bank of Nigeria data) and an estimated **$21.8 billion in 2025** [VERIFIED — Nairametrics,
Vanguard]. Global context: Sub-Saharan Africa as a whole received approximately $56 billion in
remittances in 2024 per World Bank data, while a separate source states Africa overall (including
North Africa) received over $95 billion in 2024 [VERIFIED — both figures found and reported with
their sources; the discrepancy likely reflects different regional groupings (Sub-Saharan Africa vs.
all of Africa) and is not resolved by this research]. No primary data was located quantifying what
share of diaspora remittances is specifically land- or property-directed **[NOT VERIFIED — requires
primary research, ideally via CBN or a dedicated diaspora-investment survey]**.

### Population Growth and Housing Demand

Nigeria's population was estimated at approximately 234 million by the end of 2025, trending toward
239 million in 2026 and 244 million in 2027 per Trading Economics' World-Bank-derived projections,
with a separate mid-2026 estimate of approximately 242.4 million from Worldometer [VERIFIED — both
sources report closely aligned figures with minor methodological variance].

### Land Transaction Challenges

Beyond the disputes data in Part III, this research did not locate a single consolidated dataset
quantifying average land-transaction timelines, average cost as a percentage of transaction value,
or transaction volume nationally **[NOT VERIFIED — requires primary research]**.

## Part III — Fraud Landscape

### The Clearest Available Signal

The single most concrete, quantified figure located in this research on Nigeria's land-trust deficit
is that **approximately 65% of civil cases in Nigerian courts are land-related disputes**, attributed
to the Nigerian Institute of Advanced Legal Studies (2023) and reported via legal-industry commentary
(Mondaq) [VERIFIED via secondary citation — the original NIALS publication itself was not directly
retrieved in this research pass and should be sourced and confirmed directly from NIALS before use
in an investor or government due-diligence context].

### Common Fraud Types (Qualitatively Documented, Not Statistically Quantified)

Nigerian legal-industry commentary consistently identifies the following as common land-dispute and
fraud categories, without, in the sources located, attaching a reliable frequency or economic-impact
figure to each individually:

- **Double allocation / multiple registrations** — the same parcel sold or allocated to more than
  one party.
- **Forged documents** — fabricated or altered title and survey documentation.
- **Boundary disputes** — disagreement over a parcel's actual physical extent.
- **Impersonation** — a fraudulent party posing as a legitimate landowner or their agent.
- **Illegal sales** — sale of land by a party without legitimate authority to do so.
- **Registry inconsistencies** — conflicting or duplicated records across different registry layers.
- **Survey fraud** — a fraudulent or unverified boundary submission.
- **Identity fraud** — fraudulent use of another party's identity in a land transaction.

**[NOT VERIFIED — requires primary research]:** frequency and economic-impact breakdowns by fraud
type; government or law-enforcement statistical reports specifically quantifying land fraud (as
distinct from the general 65%-of-civil-cases figure above); and academic studies with a specific,
citable land-fraud prevalence rate. This research located consistent qualitative description of
these fraud categories across multiple Nigerian legal-commentary sources, but no single
authoritative quantitative breakdown.

### Court Statistics

Beyond the 65% civil-caseload figure above, this research did not locate disaggregated Nigerian
judiciary statistics on land-case case-loads, resolution timelines, or backlog size specific to land
matters, despite qualitative press commentary (e.g., Independent Newspaper Nigeria's reporting on
land disputes "choking" the judiciary) describing the scale of the problem in strong terms without
attaching a further specific figure **[NOT VERIFIED — requires primary research, likely via direct
engagement with the National Judicial Council or state High Court registries]**.

### Methodology

The framework below follows a standard top-down TAM → SAM → SOM structure, anchored on the one
directly relevant, government/press-cited figure this research located and verified: the **$300
billion in potential capital** reported to be locked up in Nigeria's approximately 90%-unregistered

### Total Addressable Market (TAM) — Illustrative, Not Verified

**TAM framing:** if the $300 billion figure is treated as a proxy for the value of land assets
whose formalization LandVault's category of trust infrastructure could, in principle, help unlock
or support over time, this represents a ceiling-level reference point for the *underlying asset

in revenue.** [ASSUMPTION: that the $300 billion figure is a reasonable order-of-magnitude proxy for
the addressable underlying asset base; this assumption is not independently verified and should be
tested against primary land-valuation research before use in investor materials.]

### Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM) — Illustrative, Not Verified

Narrowing from the TAM proxy above to the specific services LandVault's five-layer model
monetizes (verification, marketplace transactions, enterprise subscriptions, government licensing —
Bible Volume II, Part VII) requires an assumed **take rate or service-fee ratio** applied to
underlying transaction or asset value. **No primary research was located establishing a defensible,
Nigeria-specific take rate for land-verification or land-marketplace services specifically**
[NOT VERIFIED]. Absent such a rate, this document declines to compute an illustrative SAM dollar
figure, because doing so would require inventing an unsupported ratio — the exact practice this
report's own research principles forbid. **This is itself the report's most important finding in
this Part: SAM sizing requires a dedicated primary research engagement (a commissioned market study
or data-partner engagement) before a defensible number can be produced.**

### Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) — Illustrative, Not Verified

SOM depends on SAM (above) and on assumptions about market share achievable within a defined time
horizon, professional-network growth rate (`docs/NETWORK_GROWTH_STRATEGY.md`'s own 100 → 1,000 →
10,000 → 100,000-surveyor stages), and government-partnership timing (the National Land Digital
System's own stated five-year registration horizon, which LandVault has no confirmed role in as of
this Volume's writing). **No SOM figure is presented, for the same reason as SAM above: computing
one without a verified SAM input and a confirmed go-to-market timeline would require fabricating an
unsupported number.**

### Recommendation

This Part's honest conclusion is a research requirement, not a number: **a defensible TAM/SAM/SOM
for LandVault requires a commissioned primary research engagement** — ideally combining (a) direct
engagement with SURCON/NIS for professional-population sizing, (b) a CBN/NMRC-sourced mortgage- and
lending-market sizing, and (c) a bottom-up transaction-volume model built from at least one pilot
market (e.g., Lagos State) rather than a top-down national extrapolation from the single $300
billion asset-value proxy this research was able to verify.

Also demonstrates that even a well-resourced, decades-long national programme reports its progress in stages rather than claiming instant completion — a model for honest, incremental progress reporting. |
| **Kenya — Ardhisasa (National Land Information Management System)** | Launched **27 April 2021**, a joint Ministry of Lands/National Land Commission platform; integrated with Kenya Revenue Authority, Business Registration Services, and national population/immigration registration systems; reported to have reduced processing times "from weeks or months to days" and reduced fake title deed cases [VERIFIED — dmklaw.co.ke, haofinder.com, mmsadvocates.co.ke — note these are legal-commentary/industry sources, not Kenya's own official statistics office, and the "reduced fake title deeds" claim specifically should be corroborated against an official Kenyan government source before being cited in a rigorous due-diligence context]. | Closely analogous regional (African) precedent for cross-agency government-system integration underpinning a national land platform — directly relevant to Nigeria's own National Land Digital System design. |
| **Brazil — blockchain land-registry pilot (Cartório, Pelotas/Morro Redondo)** | A pilot partnership between US-based Ubitquity and Brazilian real-estate registries (Cartório de Registro de Imóveis) in the municipalities of Pelotas and Morro Redondo, embedding property-record hashes into the Bitcoin blockchain via the Colored Coins protocol [VERIFIED — CoinDesk, 2017]. As of the sources located, **no Brazilian state regulation was found recognizing blockchain-based land registration as legally binding**, and this research did not confirm whether the pilot was ever scaled nationally [VERIFIED as a limitation — Migalhas legal commentary]. | A cautionary precedent: a technically interesting pilot without regulatory/legal-recognition follow-through does not translate into adopted infrastructure — reinforcing LandVault's own constitutional emphasis (LV-000 Article VII) on documentation, governance, and — critically — real institutional/government partnership, not technology novelty alone, as the path to genuine adoption. |

### Lessons for Nigeria, Synthesized

Three patterns recur across every genuinely successful example above (Rwanda, Estonia, UK,
Singapore, India, Kenya) and are conspicuously absent from the one clearly unsuccessful example
(Brazil's unscaled pilot): **(1) direct government ownership or sponsorship of the initiative, (2)
integration with an existing national identity system**, and **(3) a multi-year, honestly staged
delivery timeline rather than a claim of instant completion.** Nigeria's own National Land Digital
System (Part V) already exhibits pattern (1) — direct government/World Bank sponsorship — which is
the single most important precondition every successful comparator shares. Whether it achieves
patterns (2) and (3) remains to be seen and was not assessable from the sources located in this
research.

## Part XII — Strategic Implications

**Why trust infrastructure is becoming essential.** Every verified figure in this report — the
65%-of-civil-cases land-dispute rate, the 90%+ unregistered-land condition, the housing-deficit
range of 14.9–28 million units depending on methodology — points to the same underlying condition:
Nigeria's land economy is large, growing, and structurally under-served by verifiable trust
infrastructure. This is not a strategic opinion; it is the direct implication of the verified
evidence assembled above.

**Why marketplace models outperform traditional software.** This is a strategic judgment, not a
verified finding: a marketplace model that connects the professional-accreditation gap identified
in Part IV to the verification need identified in Parts II–III creates value on both sides of the
transaction simultaneously, in a way a pure software-licensing model selling to one side alone does
not. This report labels this a strategic opinion because no primary research directly comparing
marketplace-model versus software-licensing-model outcomes specifically in Nigerian land-tech was
located.

**Why platform economics apply.** The international benchmarking in Part XI shows every successful
comparator integrating multiple government systems and, in Kenya's and India's cases specifically,
national identity infrastructure — evidence that a platform model (multiple integrated layers)
outperforms a single-purpose tool, consistent with LandVault's own five-layer architecture (Bible
Volume II, Part IV). This is a reasoned inference from the verified international evidence, not
itself a new statistic.

**Why enterprise integration matters.** The mortgage-market and lending-constraint reasoning in Part
VI, while not independently quantified for Nigeria specifically, follows directly from the
well-established development-finance principle that unverifiable collateral suppresses formal
lending — a principle this report treats as reasonable inference from verified structural
conditions, not as a Nigeria-specific statistic.

**Why government partnerships matter.** Every successful international comparator in Part XI had
direct government sponsorship; Nigeria's own National Land Digital System already provides this
precondition at the national level, independent of LandVault's own current involvement (or lack
thereof) in that specific initiative. This is the report's most directly evidenced strategic
implication.

**Why APIs create long-term defensibility.** This is a strategic opinion consistent with, but not
independently verified by, the evidence in this report — it follows the same reasoning already
articulated in Bible Volume II (Part X) and is not restated as new evidence here.

**Why network effects become the competitive moat.** As with the point above, this is strategic
reasoning consistent with Bible Volume II, not a new quantitative finding of this report — the
report's genuinely new contribution is the verified evidence in Parts I–VIII and XI supporting the
scale and urgency of the underlying opportunity, not a new proof of the network-effects thesis
itself.

## Summary of Verified Evidence vs. Research Gaps

**Strongest verified evidence in this report:** Nigeria's National Land Digital System (dated, named
officials, stated targets); the 65%-land-related-civil-cases figure; Nigeria population/urbanization
data; diaspora remittance figures; the international benchmarking comparators (Rwanda, Estonia, UK,
Singapore, India, Kenya, Brazil); the African Land Policy Centre's existence and history; and
Nigeria's GDP range (with the nominal-vs-PPP distinction explicitly flagged).

**Most material research gaps, requiring dedicated primary research before this report should be
cited in a specific investment, procurement, or government-partnership decision:** (1) the total
number of SURCON-registered/licensed surveyors in Nigeria; (2) a defensible Nigerian
land-verification or marketplace take rate sufficient to compute SAM/SOM; (3) disaggregated land-
fraud frequency and economic-impact data by fraud type; (4) state-by-state registry-digitization
maturity; (5) sector-specific enterprise land-verification demand (developers, infrastructure,
extractives, agriculture); and (6) a comprehensive Nigerian mortgage-market size and penetration
figure.

**This report's own recommendation, stated plainly:** LandVault should commission dedicated primary
research — ideally in direct partnership with SURCON, NIALS, CBN/NMRC, and at least one state
Ministry of Lands — to close gaps (1) through (6) above before any of this report's illustrative
Part IX/X scenarios are presented to an investor, bank, or government partner as anything more
than illustrative planning assumptions.
